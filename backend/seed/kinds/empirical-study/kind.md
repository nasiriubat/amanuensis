# Empirical study

A paper whose contribution is evidence about how something is: a controlled experiment,
a survey, interviews, a case study, or mining of repositories or logs. The question
comes first and the method is chosen to answer it.

## What reviewers expect

- Research questions or hypotheses stated before the method.
- A method matched to the question, described in enough detail to replicate.
- Participants or data described: how many, who, how selected, why that is adequate.
- Analysis appropriate to the data: statistics with effect sizes for quantitative
  work, a named coding approach for qualitative work.
- Results reported per question, with the data shown.
- Interpretation kept separate from results.
- Threats to validity: construct, internal, external, conclusion.
- Ethics: consent, anonymisation, approval where relevant.

## Common reasons for rejection

- Questions appear after the results, fitted to what was found.
- Sample too small or too convenient for the claim made.
- Statistics misused: p-values without effect sizes, multiple comparisons uncorrected.
- Qualitative findings presented as counts without a coding method.
- Results and discussion blended so the reader cannot tell data from opinion.
- Generalising beyond the population studied.

## Framing moves that work

- Motivate the question with a concrete disagreement or an unknown that matters.
- Present the study design as a figure or a table.
- Report a finding per question with the strongest evidence first.
- State what the study does not show as plainly as what it shows.
